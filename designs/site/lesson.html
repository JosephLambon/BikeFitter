Saddle height: let your legs decide.

Why formulas only get your saddle height close, and how to fine-tune it on the bike.

Level
1, Fundamentals
Evidence
Expert practice
Last checked
6 November 2026

The idea

  • Formulas only get you close.

    Expert practice

    Formulas based on your inside leg don’t account for your foot length or pedalling style, your saddle setback, how flexible you are, your crank length, or today’s thinner shoes and lower pedals. Steve Hogg calls a formula that gets it right “a lucky accident”.

    Steve Hogg, fitter1

  • Too high is the more common mistake.

    Expert practice

    Hogg says “something like 80%” of his first-time clients leave with a lower saddle than they arrived with.

    Steve Hogg, fitter1

  • Watch the back of the knee.

    Expert practice

    Hogg judges height by how the leg straightens under real effort. If the back of the knee speeds up, even slightly, just before the bottom of the pedal stroke, the saddle is too high.

    Steve Hogg, fitter1

  • Check it again after other changes.

    Expert practice

    Saddle height “needs constant checking and reassessment after any further changes to other parameters”, such as moving your saddle or cleats.

    Steve Hogg, fitter2

Key takeaway

A formula gets you close. Your legs, under load, have the final say.

Try it

Hogg’s hill test.

A way to set your own saddle height, in Steve Hogg’s words “under significant load”: seated, forcing a gear a bit at 80–85 rpm.1

Had a professional fit? Mat Steinmetz suggests changing the saddle by no more than 2–3 mm yourself before you contact your fitter.3

  1. Measure your saddle height now and write it down. Put a strip of tape on the seatpost where it enters the frame, so you can always get back.
  2. Find a hill that takes at least three minutes to ride up, and warm up thoroughly.
  3. Ride up it seated, about one gear harder than is comfortable.
  4. Ask yourself: do you feel like you’re on a step machine, or fluent through the bottom of the pedal stroke on both legs?
  5. On a step machine, or a bit powerless? Lower the saddle 3 mm and repeat until you feel fluent.
  6. Fluent on both sides? Raise it 3 mm and repeat, until one side feels a touch less fluent than the other. Then lower it 6 mm.Steps 2–6: Steve Hogg1

Before you adjust anything. Use the torque setting printed on the seat clamp, especially with carbon parts, and never raise the seatpost above its minimum-insertion mark. Don’t make a big change just before a long ride. If you have pain or numbness, see a qualified bike fitter, a physiotherapist or your GP.
