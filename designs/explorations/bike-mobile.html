Design concepts · phones

Five ways Explore the bike could work on a phone.

Each phone is live: tap parts, drag the sliders, swipe the cards. All use the real drawing and real part copy from the prototype engine, and stay inside Workstand's rules.

The common problem they each attack: today the bike is small, the controls are three unlabelled icons, and the bottom half of the screen is empty.

1Workbench sheetMaps-style sheet

The bike above, the bench drawer below.

Everything you touch lives in one window sheet at the thumb. A segmented control replaces the three mystery icons: Together · Apart · Build. Tap a part and the sheet grows; the stage shrinks so the card never covers the drawing.

  • For: familiar phone pattern, controls labelled, all within thumb reach.
  • Against: least "Workstand-specific"; a growing sheet makes the bike small while reading.

2Parts shelfswipe to explore

Swipe through the parts like cards on a shelf.

Fingers are too big for a chain or a mech hanger, so the phone flips the job: system chips and a snap carousel of shelf cards pick the part, and the drawing lights it up. Tapping the bike still works, and scrolls the shelf to match.

  • For: no fiddly hit targets; you see there are 36 parts to discover; reads well one-handed.
  • Against: the card shows less copy at once (full text on tap); carousels hide content off-screen.

3Tool wallpegboard flat-lay

Take it apart, and hang every part on the pegboard.

The most Workstand idea: rule 2 ("tools hang on pegboard") made literal. On a phone the explode lands on a scrolling wall of small windows, one per part, grouped by system. Tap the whole bike to see where a part lives; tap a peg to read about it.

  • For: every part big and tappable; the no-overlap rule becomes trivial; very ownable.
  • Against: loses the sense of parts flying apart on the phone; more scrolling.

4Guided tourzoom to each part

Let the bike lead: next part, next part.

A story, not a toy. The drawing zooms smoothly onto each part in a fitter's order (contact points first), the copy sits underneath, and one big thumb button moves on. "Whole bike" zooms out; tapping any part jumps the tour there.

  • For: best for a newcomer; teaches in order; the bike is always big and the part always visible.
  • Against: less free play; the explode becomes a secondary mode.

5Wall hookportrait-native

Hang the bike up, the way a workshop does.

Bikes in a workshop hang by the front wheel. So in portrait the drawing does too: it turns upright and fills the tall screen, 40% bigger than today with no "turn your phone" prompt. Controls and the card follow below, in the page's own scroll.

  • For: biggest bike in portrait by far; memorable; zero new interaction to learn.
  • Against: sideways bike can feel odd to some; explode vectors were designed for the side view.
